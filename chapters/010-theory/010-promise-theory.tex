Since this thesis uses promise theory to explain several components of Kubernetes and the architecture of the developed solution, this chapter serves as a brief introduction to the concepts of promise theory.
Definitions and notation can be found in \cite{promise-theory}.
The following notation denotes a promise of the first kind, namely a direct promise from $A$ to $B$ with body $b$ \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[ A \xrightarrow{b} B \]

The body $b$ may contain a label $\Lambda (b)$ uniquely identifying the promise, a type $\tau (b)$ explaining the promise and a constraint $\chi (b)$ on the state of the agent.
Additionally, the body $b$ may be signed to indicate whether the promise is a promise to give or a promise to accept \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[ A \xrightarrow{-b} B \]

Therefore, the promise above is the promise $\Lambda (b)$ from $A$ to $B$ to accept $\tau (b)$ subject to $\chi (b)$.
\clearpage

In addition to promises, it is also important to understand the concept of an imposition, which is an attempt to induce cooperation in another agent without prior, specific consent.
Impositions may attempt to induce behavior which cannot be promised, leading to them being generally less likely to succeed. \parencite{promise-theory}
The following notation denotes an imposition \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[ \pi _1: A \impose{b}{5} B \]

Finally, the principle of autonomy is one of the fundamental elements of promise theory.
It states that no agent can be coerced into making a promise, and no agent can make promises on behalf of another.
This also means that an imposition requires a promise to accept the imposition, which may need to be made ad hoc when the imposition is made. \parencite{promise-theory}
