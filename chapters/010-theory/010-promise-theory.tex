\cite{promise-theory}, titled \enquote{Promise Theory} is a book by Mark Burgess and Jan A.
Bergstra which introduces the concept of promise theory. This chapter serves as a brief summary of the most fundamental concepts in the book.
Promise theory will be used as a baseline for describing the software architecture which the developed software will be based on.

\subsection{Defining Promises}
Promise theory is about promises, which means we need to define what a promise is.
We shall work with the definitions from \cite{promise-theory} below:

\enquote{
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textit{Intention:} A subject or type of possible behavior, i.e. something that can be interpreted to have significance. Any agent can harbour [\textit{sic}] intentions. It could be something like \textquotesingle be red\textquotesingle  for a light, or \textquotesingle win the race\textquotesingle  for a sports person.
		\item \textit{Promise:} When an intention is publicly declared to some audience (called its scope) it becomes a promise. Thus [\textit{sic}] a promise is a shared intention.
		\item \textit{Imposition:} This is an attempt to induce cooperation in another agent, i.e. to implant an intention, without prior invitation of promise to accept. It is complementary to the idea of a promise, and involves an element of the unexpected. Degrees of imposition include: hints, advice, suggestions, requests, commands, etc.
		      \par
		      . . .
		      \par
		\item \textit{Assessment:} A decision about whether a promise has been kept or not. Every agent makes its own assessment about promises it is aware of. Often assessment involves the observation of other agents\textquotesingle  behaviors.
	\end{itemize}
} (p. 4)

An agent in this context is any active entity in Promise Theory, which can include humans, computers or other objects or beings.
This means that promises set expectations for how things and people may behave. \parencite{promise-theory}

For noting promises we shall use the formal notation from \cite{promise-theory}.
The following notation denotes a promise of the first kind, namely a direct promise from $A$ to $B$ with body $b$ \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[
	A \xrightarrow{b} B
\]


The body $b$ may contain a label $\Lambda (b)$ uniquely identifying the promise, a type $\tau (b)$ explaining the promise and a constraint $\chi (b)$ on the state of the agent.
Additionally, the body $b$ may be signed to indicate whether the promise is a promise to give or a promise to accept \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[
	A \xrightarrow{-b} B
\]


Therefore, the promise above is the promise $\Lambda (b)$ from $A$ to $B$ to accept $\tau (b)$ subject to $\chi (b)$.
We will also be looking at promises of the second kind, which are noted as below \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[
	A[B] \xrightarrow{b} C
\]

The formula below denotes a promise that is made by $A$ to $C$ ob behalf of $B$, meaning that $A$ promises behavior of $B$. It should be noted that this is not a guarantee for $B$ to actually behave in that way, since $B$ has made no promise itself. Such a constellation is equivalent to an imposition, which can be denoted like this \parencite{promise-theory}:

\[
	A \impose{B \xrightarrow{b} C}{10} B
\]

Meaning $A$ imposes on $B$ that $B$ make a promise to $C$ about $b$.
